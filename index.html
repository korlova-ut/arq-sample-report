What’s stopping a good AI experience from becoming a great one?

For the most part, Meridian Assistant works: people find it useful, easy to interact with and trustworthy. The biggest opportunity appears when a request falls outside the ordinary. The assistant largely avoids making things up, but could do more to explain its limitations and help users understand what to do next by giving them more information and control when they need it most.

Read on to find out more about this and other opportunities.

Meridian Assistant delivers a solid everyday experience, with an overall ARQ Score of 71 out of 100. People generally found it useful, easy to interact with and easy to understand, and the assistant usually avoided inventing an answer when it could not fulfil a request. The clearest weakness emerged when something went wrong: people were often told they could not get what they asked for, but were rarely given a clear explanation or useful next step. The opportunity is therefore less about improving everyday interactions and more about making failure and recovery feel like part of a well-designed experience.

What the score tells us

Everyday interactions work well

People generally find Meridian Assistant useful, easy to interact with and easy to understand. This is reflected across Outcome, Understanding and Affinity, which all perform relatively strongly. Task completion also improved from 83% on the first everyday task to 97% on the final one.

Meridian Assistant knows when not to make something up

When asked for something it could not legitimately provide, Meridian Assistant declined to give a figure 95% of the time. That behaviour helps protect Trust, with 81% saying they felt confident in the information it provided.

Refusals rarely came with a reason or a next step

Meridian Assistant rarely explained why it could not help or gave people a useful way forward. This is where Control separates from the other elements. Only 35% of the 133 people who encountered a problem found it easy to get back on track, and 48% knew where else to turn.

A bad moment did not carry over to the next task

After the forced failure, the one task built around a request Meridian Assistant could not fulfil, ratings across all five elements improved on the final everyday task and finished above where they started. A poor moment does not necessarily damage the wider relationship with the assistant.

Where to focus next

Grouped by how much of the study stands behind each one. Nothing here estimates how far the score would move.

Strongest evidence

Say why the request cannot be met

When Meridian Assistant cannot fulfil a request, be clear about why. A refusal is easier to act on when people understand what is getting in the way.

Strongest evidence

Always leave people a next step

Avoid leaving the conversation at “I can’t help with that.” Where possible, suggest an alternative, provide the nearest useful answer or make the next step clear.

Strongest evidence

Make recovery easier

Give people more control when a conversation goes off track. Make it easy to retry, change direction or move on without having to start again.

Supporting evidence

Make human handoffs feel helpful

When human support is needed, explain what will happen next and why contact details are required. The handoff should feel like a continuation of the help, rather than a replacement for it.

Supporting evidence

Make the reasoning clearer

People generally understand Meridian Assistant’s answers, but are less clear on how it arrived at them. Give enough context to help people judge the answer and decide how much to rely on it.

See the experience through participants’ eyes

Watch how people responded to Meridian Assistant in the moments that worked well, and when the conversation became harder to navigate.

What sits behind the five elements

The five elements are different views of the same experience. Small differences between them should not be treated as a ranking. The useful signal is when one behaves differently from the rest.

Control does. At 60, it sits around 13 points below the middle of the other four elements. That gap comes largely from two recovery questions: only 35% found it easy to get back on track and 48% knew where else to get help.

Remove those recovery questions and Control sits much closer to the other elements. The weakness is therefore specific. People can steer Meridian Assistant reasonably well when the conversation is working. They have much less control when it is not.

The ARQ Score is the average of the five element scores. Each element combines its underlying scored questions and measures, with gated questions weighted to the people who answered them. These are the questions behind each one.

The framework in depth

How an ARQ Score is built

ARQ combines what people rated with what they did and said during the study. The result is an overall score, five diagnostic elements and the evidence needed to understand what is driving them.

Collected
21 scored ARQ questions + task completionThe same 21-question ARQ set is used in every study, which is what makes two products, two releases or the same experience over time comparable. Task completion is measured separately across the study tasks.
What people didTask completion and behaviour across realistic tasks, including a deliberately difficult scenario designed to test how the experience handles its limits.
What people saidOpen-ended responses and think-aloud feedback provide context for the ratings and show why people responded as they did.
What researchers observedResearchers review the sessions for behaviour people may not mention themselves, such as hesitation, rereading, repeated prompts or workarounds. They also connect the ratings, the behaviour and what people said into one account, and decide what the pattern means.

The output is three things at once: the score, the five elements under it, and the relationship between them. The order is the same in every ARQ report, so an element can be traced from the score through to the recommendations.

* The five scores in this figure are invented. They show what an ARQ Score looks like when it is drawn. This study's own scores are in the executive summary, and element by element in A closer look at the ARQ Score.

Why these five elements?

The five elements were defined before the final measurement model. They are grounded in existing research on human-AI collaboration, alongside foundational research into what matters when people work with AI. That work pointed to five aspects of the relationship that need to be understood together: whether people understand what the AI is doing, trust it appropriately, remain in control of the interaction, achieve the outcome they need, and develop sufficient affinity with the experience for the interaction to feel right.

We then refined how those elements are measured through multiple rounds of experimentation across different AI experiences. The experiments helped us determine which questions reliably capture each element and how much measurement each one needs. Some elements can be adequately covered with three questions; others require more, including questions that become relevant when something goes wrong. More questions do not make an element more important.

For that reason, each element contributes 20% of the ARQ Score. Equal weighting reflects our view that the quality of an AI relationship depends on all five components, while preventing elements with larger question sets from having disproportionate influence on the overall score. The result is one composite measure of AI Relationship Quality, with the five elements providing the diagnostic lens for understanding what is driving that score.

Study design

How we tested Meridian Assistant

We used three tasks in a fixed order. The first and third were everyday requests that Meridian Assistant should be able to handle. The middle task deliberately asked for something it could not provide, allowing us to see how the experience behaves when the right answer is a limitation or refusal.

Task 1Everyday

Finding out what the platform offers

Your company wants feedback from people in specialised business roles. You are evaluating this platform. Use the AI assistant to explore what it offers and whether anything could help you reach specialised audiences.

Why we included it: An everyday discovery task that establishes the baseline experience when Meridian Assistant can answer the request.

Task 2Forced failure

Pricing a survey for a specific audience

You are looking to support your international team who want to launch a new project. Using only the AI assistant, get a total price for running this survey: 200 participants in Bali, all retired and living near the beach. You will be asked to report the total cost you were given, and confirm the audience is available.

Why we included it: This task creates a deliberate failure. The requested audience is not available, so there is no legitimate total price. This lets us see whether Meridian Assistant recognises the limit, explains it clearly and gives the user a useful way forward.

Task 3Everyday, after failure

Checking whether it can help the marketing team

Last task. Your marketing team has heard you are evaluating this platform and wants to know if it could be useful for them too. Use the AI assistant to find out how it can help marketers.

Why we included it: Another everyday request, similar in difficulty to Task 1, but placed immediately after the forced failure. This shows whether the difficult interaction affects what happens next.

Method and data

The sample.
200 participants rated the experience and a separate 15 participants completed the same tasks while thinking aloud, 215 in total. All sessions were unmoderated and completed on desktop web. Both groups together include 116 participants in the United States and 99 in the United Kingdom. Every scored figure in this report comes from the 200 who rated. Cell sizes were set before fieldwork and the dataset was locked before analysis.
Quality exclusions.
Exclusion criteria were set before analysis. Participants were removed for repeat participation, never attempting the middle task, or completing the study implausibly quickly against a predefined threshold. Open responses were also read for quality, and participants were removed where the written answers were gibberish, copied from the question, or otherwise carried no meaning. Every exclusion is recorded in the study record.

The score.
Each question is scored as the percentage of participants who selected 4 or 5 on the five-point scale. The scored questions and task-level measures roll up into five elements, weighted by the number of people who answered each one. Each element contributes equally to the final ARQ Score, and the five elements combine into the composite. The bands are 0 to 45 Very poor, 46 to 60 Poor, 61 to 75 Average, 76 to 90 Good, 91 to 100 Great, adopted verbatim from QXscore, and they are interpretation bands rather than an external benchmark.

The framework, and the study

What is ARQ

ARQ is a framework that measures the quality of the relationship between a person and an AI experience, across five elements: Control, Outcome, Understanding, Trust and Affinity. Together they produce one ARQ Score out of 100.

Control

Can people steer it, correct it, and recover when it slips?

Outcome

Useful, relevant answers, and did people actually finish?

Understanding

Could people follow it and tell what it was doing?

Trust

Confidence in it, and willingness to rely on it again.

Affinity

How it felt to work with: tone, and satisfaction overall.

What we tested

200 people completed three tasks with Meridian Assistant and rated the experience on the standard ARQ question set. A further 15 went through the same journey thinking aloud.

Who they were. Business buyers and researchers, all on desktop web and unmoderated. Both groups together come to 215 people, 116 in the United States and 99 in the United Kingdom. Two of the three tasks were ordinary discovery questions. The middle one asks for a price nobody could quote, so it is in the study to see what the assistant does when it has no answer to give.

The scoring, the weighting, the interpretation bands and the exact questions people were asked are in The framework in depth, at the end of the report.
